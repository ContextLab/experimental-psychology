# Picture Lab Rubric (10 points total)

## Criterion 1: Staged reflections (2 points)

**Description:** Include all four reflection entries written at the designated moments during the lab: (1) prediction before writing instructions, (2) reflection after writing instructions, (3) reflection after following others' instructions but before seeing originals, (4) reflection after seeing originals and reproductions. Each entry should be 2–3 sentences and demonstrate that it was written at the right time — not fabricated after the fact.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 2 | Exceeds | All four entries are present and clearly written at different stages. The progression tells a story: the prediction (entry 1) names a specific aspect of the drawing they expect to be hard; entry 2 describes tradeoffs that only make sense if the student was involved in drafting; entry 3 expresses uncertainty about specific steps in other groups' instructions (without referencing the originals); entry 4 explicitly revisits the prediction and describes what actually happened. The entries show genuine evolution of thinking — e.g., "I predicted the star would be hardest to describe, but it turned out the relative positions of shapes were the real problem." |
| 1.5 | Mastery | All four entries are present and appear to be written at appropriate times. Content is specific enough to demonstrate the student participated but the connections between entries may be underdeveloped — e.g., entry 4 doesn't clearly reference entry 1's prediction. |
| 1 | Near | Three entries present, or all four present but some are generic enough that they could have been written at any time (e.g., "I thought the instructions were hard to follow" without naming which instructions or which specific parts). |
| 0.5 | Below | Only one or two entries present, or entries are clearly written after the fact (e.g., entry 3 references seeing the original drawings). |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing entirely or a single generic paragraph that doesn't distinguish the four stages. |

## Criterion 4: Analysis of effectiveness (2 points)

**Description:** Using other groups' evaluations and your own observations, discuss how effective your instructions were. Address: how many groups reproduced your drawing accurately, how many followed your instructions, how many assumptions were needed (and normalized by instruction count), and which specific aspects were effective or ineffective. Support claims with data from the evaluation spreadsheets.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 2 | Exceeds | Systematically addresses multiple dimensions of effectiveness (accuracy, step-following rate, assumption count, clarity/efficiency ratings). Goes beyond reporting numbers to interpret *why* — e.g., "Steps 3–5 were followed correctly by all groups because they used absolute positioning ('center of the page'), but steps 7–8 failed because they used relative positioning ('to the right of the previous shape') which different groups interpreted differently." Connects findings to the principles from the lecture (shared frame of reference, necessary vs. superfluous, progressive ordering). |
| 1.5 | Mastery | Reports data from evaluations and discusses effectiveness, but interpretation stays at the surface — e.g., "3 out of 4 groups got step 5 wrong" without explaining why. May not connect findings to broader principles about instruction-writing. |
| 1 | Near | Some data reported but analysis is thin. May rely on general impressions rather than specific data points. No connection to the evaluation spreadsheets. |
| 0.5 | Below | Mentions that instructions were "good" or "bad" without specific evidence. No data from evaluations referenced. |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing entirely. |

## Criterion 5: Most surprising miscommunications (2 points)

**Description:** Describe two specific failures: (a) as an instructor — one step in your instructions that you thought was clear but others got wrong, and (b) as a follower — one step in another group's instructions that you misinterpreted. For each, explain the gap between intent and interpretation, and propose a specific revision that would fix the miscommunication.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 2 | Exceeds | Both failures are identified with genuine specificity — names the exact step number, describes what was intended vs. what happened, and articulates *why* the gap occurred (not just that it did). The "as instructor" example shows self-awareness about blind spots in their own writing — e.g., "I wrote 'draw a small circle near the top' but I meant 'touching the top edge' while Group B interpreted it as 'in the upper third.' I assumed 'near' was precise, but it's actually vague." The "as follower" example shows honest acknowledgment of their own misinterpretation — not blaming the instruction writer. Both proposed revisions are specific and would plausibly fix the problem (not just "be more specific"). |
| 1.5 | Mastery | Both failures identified with reasonable specificity. Explanations of the gap are present but may lack depth. Revisions are proposed but may be somewhat generic ("add more detail"). |
| 1 | Near | Only one failure described well, or both described but without proposed revisions, or descriptions are vague about what specifically went wrong. |
| 0.5 | Below | Failures mentioned but without specificity — e.g., "Some groups got some things wrong." No proposed revisions. |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing entirely. |

## Criterion 6: GenAI reproductions (1 point)

**Description:** For each group's instructions (including your own), feed the instructions to a text-to-image AI model and include the AI-generated reproduction in your report. Discuss how AI reproductions compare to human reproductions: where the AI made the same mistakes as humans, where it did better, where it did worse, and what this reveals about how AI vs. humans interpret written instructions.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 1 | Exceeds | AI-generated images are included for all groups (or a clear explanation if a model couldn't process certain instructions). Discussion goes beyond "the AI got it wrong" to identify *patterns* — e.g., "The AI handled geometric descriptions well but struggled with relative positioning, while humans were the opposite — they inferred spatial relationships from context but sometimes got exact shapes wrong." Connects observations to broader insights about what makes instructions clear for different audiences. |
| 0.5 | Below | Some AI images included but discussion is superficial ("the AI images didn't look right") or images are included without meaningful comparison to human reproductions. |
| 0 | No evidence | No AI reproductions included, or section is missing. |

## Grading notes for TAs

- **AI-proofing**: The staged reflections (Criterion 1) and miscommunications (Criterion 5) are the strongest AI-proof elements. Pay special attention to whether reflections reference specific steps, specific groups, and specific moments that only someone who participated would know. The progression across the four reflection entries should show genuine evolution — if all four read like they were written at the same time, deduct points.
- **Figures**: Figure 1 (3 panels) is required. At least one additional analytical figure is required (Criterion 3). Screenshots of raw spreadsheets don't count as analytical figures.
- **Connection to principles**: Top-scoring responses in Criterion 4 should connect their findings to the three principles from the lecture (shared frame of reference, necessary vs. superfluous details, progressive ordering). This isn't required for passing but distinguishes "mastery" from "exceeds."
- **Specificity over length**: A concise report that names specific step numbers, specific groups, and specific failures is worth more than a long report that speaks in generalities. When in doubt, ask: "Could this sentence have been written by someone who wasn't in the room?" If yes, it's too generic.
- **GenAI reproductions (Criterion 6)**: Students should include AI images for all groups. If a model refused or couldn't process the instructions, the student should explain what happened — that itself is informative about instruction clarity.
