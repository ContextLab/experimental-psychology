# Pitch Session Lab Rubric (10 points total)

## Criterion 1: Pitch diary (1.5 points)

**Description:** Include both diary entries from Steps 2.5 and 4.5 of the lab. The "before" entry (written before presentations) should predict what you personally find compelling about your group's idea and what you're excited or nervous about. The "after" entry (written after all presentations) should describe what surprised you, whether any pitch changed your thinking, and how you feel about your own pitch in retrospect.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 1.5 | Exceeds | Both entries are included and clearly written at different times (the "before" entry doesn't reference events from the presentations; the "after" entry references specific moments that happened during the pitches). Entries show genuine self-awareness — the student names specific emotions, predictions, or reactions that are personal and couldn't be written by someone who wasn't there. The "after" entry explicitly addresses how their perspective shifted (or didn't) relative to the "before" entry. |
| 1 | Mastery | Both entries are included and appear to be written at the appropriate times. Content is specific enough to demonstrate the student was present but may lack depth in connecting the two entries or reflecting on how their perspective changed. |
| 0.5 | Below | Only one entry is included, OR both entries are present but are generic/vague enough that they could describe any pitch session (e.g., "I was nervous" without saying what about; "The pitches were interesting" without naming specifics). OR the "before" entry clearly references events that happened during presentations, suggesting it was written after the fact. |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing entirely, or entries are clearly fabricated (e.g., both written in the same voice at the same time with no distinction between before/after perspectives). |

## Criterion 2: What was hardest? (1 point)

**Description:** Describe the most challenging part of creating and delivering your group's pitch. Identify a specific challenge, explain why it was difficult, and describe what you would do differently next time.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 1 | Exceeds | Identifies a specific, non-obvious challenge (not just "presenting is scary" or "we didn't have enough time"). Explains *why* this particular thing was hard in a way that demonstrates genuine reflection — e.g., "We struggled to explain why our topic matters without oversimplifying, because the background requires understanding X, which we couldn't assume our audience knew." The proposed change is concrete and actionable. |
| 0.5 | Below | Challenge identified is generic or surface-level (e.g., "It was hard to stay under 5 minutes"). Little insight into *why* it was hard. The "what I'd do differently" is vague ("practice more") rather than specific. |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing, or response is a single sentence with no reflection. |

---

## Criterion 3: A moment that changed your mind (1.5 points)

**Description:** Describe a specific moment during another group's pitch where your opinion shifted — you became more interested, more skeptical, or saw something in a new way. Identify which group, what the moment was, what you thought before vs. after, and what caused the shift.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 1.5 | Exceeds | Identifies a specific group and a specific moment (not just "their whole pitch was good"). Articulates a clear before/after in the student's thinking — e.g., "I initially thought group B's topic was too niche, but when they showed that it connects to everyday decision-making, I realized it's actually more relatable than my own group's topic." The explanation of *what caused* the shift demonstrates that the student was actively processing the pitch, not just passively watching. |
| 1 | Mastery | Identifies a specific group and moment, and describes a shift in thinking, but the explanation of why is less developed. The student demonstrates they were paying attention but may not articulate the deeper reason the moment was persuasive. |
| 0.5 | Below | Vague — e.g., "Group C had a really interesting pitch that made me think differently." No specific moment identified, or the shift described is trivial ("I didn't know about this topic before"). |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing, or response doesn't describe a moment of changed thinking at all. |

## Criterion 4: Predictions vs. reality (3 points)

**Description:** Compare your group's predictions (from Step 5 of the lab) to the actual ratings data. Include at least one figure, discuss where predictions were wrong and why, and demonstrate genuine engagement with the data — not just restating numbers but interpreting what they mean.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 3 | Exceeds | Includes a clear, well-labeled figure that effectively communicates the comparison between predicted and actual ratings. Identifies specific prediction errors (not just "we were wrong about group A") and offers plausible, thoughtful explanations for *why* the prediction was wrong — e.g., "We predicted group B would score highest on clarity because their topic was simple, but they actually scored lowest — probably because they used a lot of technical jargon despite the simple underlying idea." Shows evidence of statistical thinking (e.g., noting sample size limitations, discussing whether differences are meaningful vs. noise, comparing patterns across dimensions). Discussion connects the data to broader insights about what makes communication effective. |
| 2 | Mastery | Includes a figure and discusses prediction errors with some explanation of why. The analysis is competent but may lack depth — e.g., noting that predictions were wrong without offering convincing explanations, or describing the data without connecting it to broader insights about communication. Figure is present but may have minor labeling issues. |
| 1 | Below | Figure is missing or unreadable, OR predictions are mentioned but not systematically compared to actual data. Discussion stays at the surface level — e.g., "Group A scored higher than we expected" with no explanation of why. Little evidence of statistical thinking. |
| 0 | No evidence | No figure, no predictions discussed, or section is missing entirely. |

## Criterion 5: Pitch vs. idea (2 points)

**Description:** Choose the pitch or idea you think was best (it could be your group's, but it doesn't need to be). This is intentionally ambiguous — a great pitch might sell a mediocre idea, and a brilliant idea might be undermined by a weak presentation. Reflect on whether you're evaluating the quality of the idea or the quality of the pitch, and how (or whether) those can be separated. Support your argument with evidence from the ratings data.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 2 | Exceeds | Explicitly engages with the pitch-vs-idea ambiguity in a substantive way — not just acknowledging it exists, but demonstrating that the student thought carefully about it. For example: "I chose Group C's pitch as 'best' even though Group A's *idea* was more interesting, because Group C's delivery made me actually care about a topic I would have dismissed. This raises the question of whether 'best' in science means the strongest question or the clearest communication — and I think in practice it has to be both." Supports the choice with specific evidence from both the data and personal experience. Considers and responds to potential counterarguments. |
| 1.5 | Mastery | Acknowledges the ambiguity and makes a clear choice with supporting evidence. May lean more on data or more on personal opinion, but demonstrates genuine engagement with the question. Doesn't just pick the highest-rated group — shows reasoning. |
| 1 | Near | Makes a choice but doesn't meaningfully engage with the pitch-vs-idea distinction. Argument relies primarily on ratings data without personal interpretation, or primarily on opinion without data. |
| 0.5 | Below | Choice is stated but poorly justified. Little or no engagement with the ambiguity. No data support. |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing, or states a choice with no justification at all. |

## Criterion 6: GenAI reflection (1 point)

**Description:** Describe how you used generative AI during this lab. Address what you used it for, where it helped, where it fell short, and whether you leveraged it to its full potential. Reflect on what you could do to use GenAI more effectively in the future.

| Points | Rating | Description |
|-|-|-|
| 1 | Exceeds | Describes specific, concrete uses of GenAI (not just "I used Claude to help with my report"). Identifies at least one way AI was genuinely helpful and at least one way it was unhelpful or gave bad advice. Shows critical evaluation — e.g., "The AI suggested using a chi-square test but our data is continuous, so that didn't make sense. I learned to always check whether the AI's statistical suggestions match my actual data type." Includes forward-looking reflection on how they'd use AI differently next time. |
| 0.5 | Below | Mentions using GenAI but description is generic ("It helped me write better"). No critical evaluation of AI output quality. No specific examples of what worked or didn't. |
| 0 | No evidence | Missing, or a single sentence like "I used ChatGPT." |

## Grading notes for TAs

- **AI-proofing**: The diary entries (Criterion 1), "moment that changed your mind" (Criterion 3), and predictions (Criterion 4) are the strongest AI-proof elements. Pay special attention to whether these contain details that could only come from someone who was physically present during the presentations. Generic responses that could describe any pitch session should receive lower scores.
- **Figures**: At least one figure is required (Criterion 4). Figures should be clearly labeled with axes, titles, and referenced in the text. Figures that appear to be screenshots of raw data without interpretation don't count.
- **Writing quality**: Writing quality is not separately scored — it's baked into each criterion via the "clearly written" and "articulates" language. Students who write clearly will naturally score higher because their reasoning is visible.
- **Length**: Not a criterion. A concise 1.5-page report that addresses all criteria deeply can earn full marks. A 5-page report that is vague and generic should not.
